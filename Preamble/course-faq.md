# Course FAQ

## What will class actually look like?

The lecture portion of PHYS 1401 meets three days each week. Most class meetings combine explanation, demonstrations, and guided worked examples.

There will be selected opportunities to solve problems individually or in small groups, but the course is not taught entirely in a studio format. Bring a scientific calculator and be prepared to draw diagrams, estimate quantities, and attempt parts of a problem during class.

## How does the laboratory fit into the course?

The laboratory meets as a separate section with its own schedule and assignments.

Lecture focuses primarily on physical principles, models, and problem solving. Laboratory focuses on measurement, data, graphs, uncertainty, and comparison between experimental results and theoretical predictions. Topics may connect closely, but the lecture and lab schedules will not always match exactly.

## What does a complete solution look like?

A complete solution should normally include:

1. A brief statement of what must be determined
2. A diagram, coordinate system, or description of the physical model when appropriate
3. The known and unknown quantities
4. The physical principle or general equation being used
5. Algebraic work before numerical substitution
6. Numerical values substituted with units
7. A final answer with appropriate units and significant figures
8. A conclusion explaining what the answer means and whether it is reasonable

A page of unexplained equations is not a complete solution, even when the final number is correct.

## Will I need Python or another programming language?

No. Python and programming assignments are not required in PHYS 1401.

The course notes may contain computer-generated figures, animations, or links to simulations, but these are provided to help you visualize the physics. Your submitted problem solving will focus on diagrams, algebra, trigonometry, units, graphs, and written reasoning.

## Do I need calculus?

No. PHYS 1401 is an algebra- and trigonometry-based physics course.

You will use ideas such as slope, area, and rate of change when interpreting graphs, but you will not be expected to take derivatives or evaluate integrals. Algebra, geometry, vectors, scientific notation, and right-triangle trigonometry are the main mathematical tools.

## Can I work with other students?

Yes, within the limits described for each type of assignment.

- **In class:** You may be asked to discuss or solve selected problems with other students.
- **Homework:** You may discuss concepts and general strategies, but the submitted reasoning and calculations must be your own.
- **Laboratory:** Follow the collaboration and submission rules given by your laboratory instructor.
- **Quizzes and exams:** Work independently unless explicitly told otherwise.

Copying a completed solution does not help you learn the process and may violate the academic-integrity policy.

## Can I use AI tools?

Use AI tools only in ways allowed by the course syllabus and the instructions for a particular assignment.

Appropriate study uses may include:

- asking for a simpler explanation of a concept,
- generating additional practice questions,
- checking vocabulary,
- comparing two problem-solving approaches,
- or getting feedback on whether your written explanation is clear.

Do not submit AI-generated solutions, calculations, or explanations as though they were your own work. AI output can also contain convincing mathematical and physical errors, so it must be checked against the course notes, textbook, and class instruction.

## Can I use NotebookLM to study with the course notes?

Yes. [NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com/) may be used as a study-support tool unless a particular assignment says otherwise.

You may use it to:

- summarize a section of the notes,
- generate review questions,
- create an audio overview,
- identify important vocabulary,
- make a study guide,
- or ask for another explanation of a difficult idea.

The written notes, worked examples, assigned problems, laboratory instructions, and class discussions remain the authoritative course materials. When an AI-generated explanation disagrees with them, rely on the course materials and ask for clarification.

You may not submit NotebookLM-generated text as your own reasoning in homework, laboratory work, quizzes, reflections, or exams.

### What are useful NotebookLM prompts for this course?

- Summarize this section in plain language while keeping the important physics vocabulary.
- List the most important equations and explain when each one should be used.
- Generate five conceptual questions that test understanding rather than memorization.
- Create a short quiz, but do not show the answers until after the questions.
- Explain the physical model used in this worked example.
- Identify the assumptions made in this example.
- List common mistakes students make with this topic.
- Turn this section into a study guide for an exam.
- Give me a new practice problem that uses the same principle but different numbers.

Use these prompts to strengthen your understanding, not to replace your own work.

## How are grades weighted?

The course syllabus and the gradebook in the learning-management system provide the authoritative grading weights, deadlines, and policies.

Lecture and laboratory requirements may be listed separately. Check both parts of the course rather than assuming that one schedule applies to everything.

## What calculator should I use?

A standard scientific calculator is appropriate. You should be able to enter scientific notation, powers, square roots, and trigonometric functions in both degrees and radians.

Unless an assessment says otherwise, graphing and computer-algebra features are not necessary.

## What if I start falling behind?

Act early rather than waiting for the next exam.

- Review a small amount after each class meeting.
- Attempt problems before looking at completed solutions.
- Mark the exact step where your reasoning breaks down.
- Ask questions during class or office hours.
- Use tutoring and other course-support resources.
- Keep lecture and laboratory deadlines on the same calendar.

This course rewards steady practice much more than last-minute cramming.
