# College Physics (PHYS 1401)

This repository contains the **Jupyter Book course notes, worked examples, practice problems, and study resources** for **PHYS 1401: College Physics** at [East Texas A&M University](https://www.etamu.edu/physics/).

PHYS 1401 is an introductory, algebra- and trigonometry-based physics course intended primarily for students in majors outside physics and engineering. The course emphasizes conceptual understanding, quantitative reasoning, and the application of physics to practical situations.

The book is designed as a **structured companion to the course** rather than a replacement for lecture, laboratory, or assigned problem solving. It includes:

* Conceptual explanations and physical models
* Worked examples with step-by-step reasoning
* Dimensional analysis and careful treatment of units
* Diagrams, graphs, demonstrations, and visualizations
* Practice problems for individual and in-class work
* Connections between lecture topics and laboratory measurements

The primary conceptual framework is based on [OpenStax: *College Physics 2e*](https://openstax.org/details/books/college-physics-2e), but the presentation, examples, and exercises have been reorganized and rewritten to emphasize:

* identifying the relevant physical principles,
* translating diagrams and descriptions into mathematical models,
* using algebra and trigonometry correctly,
* maintaining units throughout a calculation,
* and interpreting whether a result is physically reasonable.

## How to Use This Book

### For students

* Read the assigned sections before or soon after lecture.
* Work through examples on paper rather than only reading the solutions.
* Identify the known and unknown quantities before selecting an equation.
* Draw diagrams and choose coordinate systems when appropriate.
* Use the notes as a reference while preparing for homework, quizzes, and exams.
* Review the figures, animations, videos, and demonstrations that accompany each topic.
* Use digital study tools, including [NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com/), only in ways permitted by the course AI policy.

Many worked examples use a consistent structure:

* **The Problem**: What information is given, and what must be determined?
* **The Model**: What physical principles and assumptions describe the situation?
* **The Math**: How can algebra, trigonometry, graphs, or proportional reasoning be used to find the result?
* **The Conclusion**: What does the result mean, and is it physically reasonable?

This structure reflects the reasoning students are expected to demonstrate on homework, quizzes, laboratory assignments, and exams.

### For instructors

The material is modular and can be adapted for:

* traditional lecture courses,
* lecture courses with limited in-class group problem solving,
* courses with separate laboratory sections,
* or other algebra- and trigonometry-based introductory physics sequences.

Worked examples are written in a consistent **Problem → Model → Math → Conclusion** format that can be reused, modified, or assigned for additional practice.

## Course Structure

The lecture portion of PHYS 1401 meets three days each week. Class meetings combine explanations, demonstrations, worked examples, and selected opportunities for individual or small-group problem solving.

The course is not taught entirely in a studio format. Most instruction occurs through lecture and guided examples, but students are still expected to participate actively by drawing diagrams, estimating quantities, discussing physical reasoning, and attempting problems during class.

The laboratory is a separate course section with its own schedule and assignments. Laboratory activities emphasize:

* measurement and experimental design,
* data organization and graphing,
* uncertainty and sources of error,
* comparison between measurements and theoretical predictions,
* and clear communication of experimental results.

## Mathematical and Technical Tools

This course uses algebra, geometry, trigonometry, and graphical reasoning. Calculus and programming are not required.

Students should be prepared to work with:

* scientific notation and powers of ten,
* dimensional analysis and unit conversions,
* algebraic equations,
* ratios and proportional reasoning,
* right-triangle trigonometry,
* vectors and vector components,
* slopes and areas on graphs,
* and appropriate use of a scientific calculator.

Python is not part of the required coursework, and students are not expected to complete programming assignments. Any computer-generated plots, animations, or simulations included in the book are provided to support visualization and conceptual understanding.

Analytical reasoning remains central. A complete solution should normally include the physical principle being used, the general equation, algebraic work, numerical substitution with units, and an interpretation of the result.
