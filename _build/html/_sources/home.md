# Welcome to College Physics

This Jupyter Book contains the course notes, worked examples, practice problems, and study resources for **PHYS 1401: College Physics** at East Texas A&M University.

College Physics is an introductory, algebra- and trigonometry-based course intended primarily for students majoring in fields outside physics and engineering. The course focuses on developing a practical understanding of motion, forces, energy, momentum, rotation, fluids, and other foundational ideas in physics.

These notes are designed to help you **learn physics by doing physics**. You will read explanations, study worked examples, interpret graphs and diagrams, and practice solving problems. The goal is not simply to memorize formulas. The goal is to recognize the physical principles involved, choose an appropriate model, and explain what your answer means.

---

## How to use this book

Each chapter combines conceptual explanations with worked examples and practice problems.

Many worked examples follow the same structure:

* **The Problem**: What information is given, and what must be determined?
* **The Model**: What physical principles and assumptions describe the situation?
* **The Math**: How can the problem be solved using algebra, trigonometry, graphs, or proportional reasoning?
* **The Conclusion**: What does the result mean physically, and is it reasonable?

This structure is intentional. It reflects the problem-solving process you will be expected to use on homework, quizzes, and exams.

When studying a worked example, do not focus only on the final equation. Pay attention to why a particular physical principle was selected, how the diagram was interpreted, and how the result was checked.

## Using the course notes

The notes are meant to support the class rather than replace it.

For each chapter, you should:

1. Read the assigned sections before or soon after they are discussed in class.
2. Review the worked examples without immediately looking at the solution.
3. Write down the quantities that are known and unknown.
4. Draw a diagram when one would help describe the situation.
5. Attempt the practice problems on paper.
6. Check your units, signs, significant figures, and whether the result is physically reasonable.

Some sections include figures, animations, videos, interactive demonstrations, or short questions. Use these resources to strengthen your understanding of the physical situation before beginning the mathematical work.

You are not expected to learn Python or complete programming assignments in this course.

## Lecture and in-class work

The lecture portion of the course meets three days each week.

Class meetings will include explanations, demonstrations, worked examples, and opportunities to solve selected problems individually or in small groups. You should bring a calculator and be prepared to write, draw diagrams, estimate quantities, and discuss your reasoning.

The course is not taught entirely in a studio format, but active participation is still important. Watching someone else solve a problem is not the same as solving it yourself.

---

## Laboratory

The laboratory is a separate section of the course.

In lab, you will investigate physical principles through measurement, observation, data analysis, and comparison with theoretical predictions. Laboratory activities will often connect to topics discussed in lecture, but the lab has its own schedule, assignments, and expectations.

Success in lab requires more than obtaining a numerical result. You should also be able to explain what was measured, how uncertainty affects the result, and whether the evidence supports the expected physical model.

---

## Mathematical background

This course uses algebra, geometry, and trigonometry. Calculus and programming are not required.

You should be prepared to work with:

* Scientific notation and powers of ten
* Unit conversions
* Rearranging algebraic equations
* Ratios and proportional reasoning
* Right-triangle trigonometry
* Vectors and their components
* Slopes, areas, and basic graph interpretation

These skills will be reviewed when they are needed, but regular practice is essential. In physics, the mathematics is a tool for describing relationships between measurable quantities.

---

## What successful problem solving looks like

A strong solution usually includes more than a numerical answer. It should show:

* A clear diagram or coordinate system when appropriate
* The relevant known and unknown quantities
* The physical principle or general equation being used
* Algebraic work before numerical substitution
* Values substituted with units
* A final answer with appropriate units and significant figures
* A brief statement explaining what the result means

Writing down equations without explaining how they apply to the situation can make even correct work difficult to follow. Organizing your reasoning will help you identify mistakes and receive useful feedback.
